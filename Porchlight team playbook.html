G-01The call

Build Porchlight. Type a Texas address and get a calm, plain answer to three questions: how often does the power go out here, for how long, and would a Base Core carry this home through it? A real public-data pipeline sits underneath, and a second screen shows Base's growth team where a Core pays twice: for the homeowner during outages and for the grid during scarcity.

Tracks
Most Commercializable as the primary entry, Open Grid Data as the second. Skip Orchestration.
Who it's for
Texas homeowners in Base territory deciding whether backup power is worth it (Sally and Thomas), and the Base team that has to find and convince them.
The problem
Base's compare page answers "will I save?" Nothing answers "will my lights stay on?" for a specific address, season and home. The backup figure people hear, roughly 36 to 72 hours for one or two Cores, swings hard with August air conditioning or February electric heat.
Why it wins
It completes Base's own funnel, it runs on data Base engineers respect (15-minute outage history, ERCOT load profiles on the actual storm dates, load-zone prices), and it ships as an API plus an embeddable component Base could put on its site tomorrow.
Alternatives we considered and dropped
  • A pure ERCOT dashboard. Every other team will build one, and a chart of public prices scores low on insight.
  • A savings calculator. Base already runs one on its compare page and published a 7,148-bill study behind it.
  • A Track 2 fleet-dispatch simulator. Great fit for Base's ADER work, but it abandons our consumer direction and needs power-systems depth we can't fake in 41 hours.

P-01Porchlight, the product

Working name. Base's own photos feature porches, kids and lit windows, so it should feel at home next to their site. Swap it freely.

The flow

  1. Enter an address, or tap a persona chip (Sally, Thomas, or a third area).
  2. We detect the utility and show what Base offers there: Energy + Backup, backup through a utility program, or energy only. The user confirms the utility.
  3. Optional details: typical monthly kWh, whether the home heats with electricity, and a "must stay on" list (fridge, CPAP, well pump) that turns into kilowatts in the browser.
  4. The report renders in under a second; the plain-language summary streams in after.
  5. One next step: see Base's plans for this utility.

The report, block by block

  • Outlook. A weather-style level for homes in this county, "a 12-hour-plus outage about once every N years," the uncertainty, and how many years of data sit behind it.
  • Past storms, replayed. Every long outage in the county since 2018. Open one to see how long homes stayed dark (a band, not a single number) against a Core's energy curve for that exact week. This is the hero chart.
  • Backup by month. Hours for one and two Cores across the year, with toggles for electric heat and storm mode.
  • Right size. One or two Cores, with the reason in one sentence.
  • Right now. Active weather alerts at the address and a small ERCOT snapshot, each with an as-of time.
  • Summary and next step. About 120 words at a 7th-grade reading level, the link to Base, and a collapsible "what we assumed."

Principles

  • Heavy offline, light online. Everything expensive (15-minute outage history, event extraction, model fitting, grid value) runs in make data and lands in one DuckDB file. The API looks things up, runs a small simulation and calls two live services.
  • One contract. Pydantic models in schemas.py are the source of truth; the TypeScript client is generated from the OpenAPI spec, so the front end can't drift.
  • Everything degrades. Each external call gets a timeout, one retry with jitter, a circuit breaker and a last-known-good cache. The report carries sources[] with status and as-of time, so the UI says "alerts unavailable" instead of failing.
  • No raw addresses at rest. Geocode, keep county and tract, store a salted hash.

usable_kwh  = 39.2 * cores * (start_charge - reserve) * efficiency   # reserve and efficiency: ask Base

Outage events and per-home durations

An event is a run of 15-minute readings with customers out at or above max(50, 0.1% of modeled customers), merging gaps under an hour and dropping events under 30 minutes. Keep thresholds in config.

EAGLE-I only says how many customers are out, not who. Replay each event as a queue: rises are homes going dark, falls are homes coming back. Restoring in first-out-first-back order and in last-out-first-back order gives two different duration distributions with identical customer-hours, so SAIDI-style totals stay exact and the true tail sits between the bounds. Report both as a band.

Metric: expected 12-hour-plus outages per year for a typical home in the county, the sum over events of the share of customers dark 12 hours or longer, divided by years of usable coverage from 2018. Backtest: fit on 2018 to 2022, score 2023 to 2024 per county with Poisson deviance and rank correlation against two baselines, the statewide mean and the raw county rate. Ship the chart whatever it says.

Starter code: per-home durations and coverage (tested on a synthetic storm) 
from collections import deque
import numpy as np

def customer_durations(customers_out, dt_h=0.25, order="fifo"):
    """Split one county outage event into per-customer outage durations (hours).

    EAGLE-I only reports how many customers are out every 15 minutes. Treat rises as
    homes going dark and falls as homes restored. The "fifo" and "lifo" orderings give
    two bounds on the tail; customer-hours are identical under both.
    """
    c = np.asarray(customers_out, dtype=float)
    steps = np.diff(np.concatenate(([0.0], c, [0.0])))  # open and close the event at zero
    waiting = deque()                                   # [step_went_dark, customers]
    durations, weights = [], []
    for t, delta in enumerate(steps):
        if delta > 0:
            waiting.append([t, delta])
            continue
        restored = -delta
        while restored > 1e-9 and waiting:
            batch = waiting[0] if order == "fifo" else waiting[-1]
            take = min(batch[1], restored)
            durations.append((t - batch[0]) * dt_h)
            weights.append(take)
            batch[1] -= take
            restored -= take
            if batch[1] <= 1e-9:
                if order == "fifo":
                    waiting.popleft()
                else:
                    waiting.pop()
    return np.array(durations), np.array(weights)


def coverage(durations, weights, backup_h):
    """Share of affected homes fully covered, and share of their dark hours covered."""
    homes = weights[durations <= backup_h].sum() / weights.sum()
    hours = (weights * np.minimum(durations, backup_h)).sum() / (weights * durations).sum()
    return homes, hours
Starter code: empirical-Bayes outlook (tested on overdispersed synthetic counties) 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def shrink_rates(events, years, floor_cv=0.25):
    """Gamma-Poisson empirical Bayes: pull noisy county rates toward their zone mean.

    events: effective long-outage count per county (fractional is fine)
    years:  years of usable coverage per county
    Returns posterior mean rate and a 90% interval.
    """
    events, years = np.asarray(events, float), np.asarray(years, float)
    rate = events / years
    mean = np.average(rate, weights=years)
    raw_var = np.average((rate - mean) ** 2, weights=years)
    noise = mean * np.average(1.0 / years, weights=years)       # expected Poisson share
    var_between = max(raw_var - noise, (floor_cv * mean) ** 2)   # floor keeps the prior honest
    beta = mean / var_between
    alpha = mean * beta
    a_post, b_post = alpha + events, beta + years
    lo, hi = stats.gamma.ppf([[0.05], [0.95]], a_post, scale=1.0 / b_post)
    return a_post / b_post, lo, hi

On 40 synthetic counties with gamma-distributed true rates, shrinkage cut mean squared error from 0.103 to 0.031 and the 90% interval covered the truth 97% of the time. Fit it separately per weather zone on the real data.
